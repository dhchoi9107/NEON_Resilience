## Title

**Static canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity encode distinct dimensions of forest biodiversity across North America**

---

## Research objectives

- **O1 — Static canopy state.** How the structural and spectral components of static canopy state contribute — in relative and unique terms — to alpha versus beta diversity.
- **O2 — Structural dynamics.** Whether structural dynamics (multitemporal LiDAR change) provide additional biodiversity information beyond static canopy state, and whether these dynamics vary systematically with stand age (succession: aggradation→gap-phase) and disturbance regime (press vs pulse).
- **O3 — Ecosystem productivity.** Whether independently estimated ecosystem productivity (MODIS MOD17, PML-V2, eddy-covariance GPP) contributes information about diversity beyond canopy state, dynamics, and broad climate, and the form of the productivity–diversity relationship.
- **O4 — Contextual modulation.** How disturbance, surrounding land use, and stand age modify these relationships.

---

## 1. Introduction

Understanding why tree diversity varies across forested landscapes is a central objective of ecology and biogeography and a prerequisite for monitoring biodiversity under accelerating global change. Tree diversity itself is multidimensional. Local communities differ not only in the number of species they contain (alpha diversity), but also in how species composition changes across space (beta diversity), and these dimensions need not respond to the same ecological processes. Field inventories provide detailed observations of species composition but are necessarily sparse in space, whereas remote sensing offers spatially continuous and repeatable measurements of forest canopies across regional to continental extents. The central challenge is therefore not simply whether remote sensing predicts biodiversity, but which dimensions of biodiversity different remotely sensed signals represent.

Remote sensing captures forests through multiple ecological dimensions rather than through a single observation. Static canopy properties describe the current structural and spectral state of vegetation. Repeated observations characterize structural dynamics, reflecting recent growth, mortality, disturbance, and recovery. Ecosystem productivity represents the functional performance of forests through realized carbon uptake. These dimensions correspond broadly to forest state, process, and function, respectively. Although they are partially correlated, each may contain distinct ecological information and therefore contribute differently to explaining biodiversity. We evaluate this proposition across temperate and boreal forests spanning the U.S. National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON).

Static canopy state has long been the principal remote-sensing window on biodiversity. Airborne LiDAR resolves canopy height, vertical layering, foliage distribution, and gap structure, representing the physical architecture within which tree species partition resources (MacArthur & MacArthur 1961; Bergen et al. 2009; Davies & Asner 2014). Spectral vegetation indices summarize variation in canopy reflectance associated with leaf pigments, water content, and canopy density (Palmer et al. 2002; Rocchini et al. 2010). Although these measurements are frequently combined, they need not provide redundant ecological information. Because compositional turnover among forest stands is often accompanied by changes in canopy architecture, structural attributes may be particularly informative about beta diversity, whereas spectral properties may more strongly reflect local canopy condition and therefore alpha diversity. Whether structural and spectral observations provide complementary information, and whether their relative importance differs between alpha and beta diversity, remains incompletely resolved.

The temporal dimension of forest structure has received comparatively less attention in biodiversity studies. Repeated LiDAR observations quantify changes in canopy height, density, vertical organization, and gap formation, thereby distinguishing forests with similar present-day structure but different developmental trajectories. Such trajectories may reflect differences in succession, disturbance history, and structural reorganization that are not captured by static canopy state alone. Consequently, structural dynamics may provide additional information about biodiversity, particularly compositional turnover, beyond that contained in average canopy structure. This possibility is especially relevant because forest succession and disturbance are fundamentally dynamic processes. Younger stands typically undergo rapid structural development, whereas mature forests increasingly experience gap formation and fine-scale structural reorganization (Bormann & Likens 1979; Franklin et al. 2002). Likewise, chronic and discrete disturbances generate contrasting temporal patterns of canopy change. We therefore ask whether structural dynamics contribute independent information about biodiversity beyond static canopy state and whether these dynamics vary systematically with stand age and disturbance regime.

Forest productivity provides a complementary functional perspective. Species–energy theory predicts that greater energy availability can support more individuals and potentially more species (Wright 1983; Currie 1991), yet remote-sensing evaluations have often relied on vegetation greenness as an indirect proxy for productivity. Because greenness reflects canopy density and phenology rather than carbon assimilation itself, we instead use independent estimates of gross primary productivity (GPP) derived from satellite-constrained process models and eddy-covariance observations. Rather than treating productivity as an alternative to structural or spectral information, we evaluate whether realized ecosystem carbon uptake contributes additional information about tree diversity beyond canopy state and dynamics.

Finally, even if these remote-sensing dimensions capture complementary aspects of biodiversity, their relationships are unlikely to be constant across ecological contexts. Disturbance, surrounding land-use conditions, and stand age influence species turnover, canopy development, and forest structural organization, and may therefore modify how strongly remotely sensed signals correspond to biodiversity. Accounting for such context dependence is essential if remotely sensed biodiversity indicators are to be transferable across heterogeneous forest landscapes.

Using standardized tree inventories from NEON together with airborne LiDAR, multispectral imagery, repeated LiDAR observations, and independent estimates of GPP, we develop multiscale hierarchical models that partition variation across domains, sites, and plots. Specifically, we ask: (O1) whether structural and spectral components of static canopy state contribute differently to alpha and beta diversity; (O2) whether structural dynamics provide additional biodiversity information beyond static canopy state; (O3) whether independently estimated ecosystem productivity contributes complementary information beyond canopy state and dynamics; and (O4) how disturbance, surrounding land use, and stand age modify these relationships. We find that the dominant remote-sensing signal differs among diversity components: canopy structure carries the largest unique contribution to local (alpha) diversity and is the only static dimension associated with compositional turnover and richness differences, while spectral greenness adds a smaller, alpha-specific signal; structural dynamics contribute most uniquely to richness differences (nestedness); and ecosystem productivity contributes most to compositional turnover, although this signal is weak and product-dependent. Because inference rests on 19 sites, we base these conclusions on a descriptive variance partition corroborated by few-cluster-valid (wild cluster bootstrap) tests, and we show that the productivity–diversity and structure–diversity associations are themselves modulated by stand age and disturbance.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Study sites
We analyzed 19 forested sites of the U.S. National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON) across the conterminous United States, spanning temperate and boreal biomes and organized within NEON's eco-climatic domains. Sites range from evergreen-needleleaf forests of the Pacific Northwest and Rocky Mountains to deciduous-broadleaf and mixed forests of the eastern United States, encompassing broad gradients in climate, productivity, stand age, and species composition. Analyses were conducted at the level of NEON base and distributed plots and aggregated to the site and domain levels as appropriate (below).

### 2.2 Tree diversity (response)
Tree species composition was derived from NEON standardized woody-vegetation inventories (DP1.10098). To maximize temporal completeness of species lists, observations were pooled across survey years at the plot level, and plots were retained only where sample coverage ≥ 0.90 to control for uneven sampling completeness. We characterized two dimensions of diversity. **Alpha diversity** was quantified as Hill numbers of order 1 (exponential of Shannon entropy; Hill q1) and order 2 (inverse Simpson; Hill q2), emphasizing common and dominant species respectively. **Beta diversity** was quantified as local contributions to beta diversity (LCBD) computed from a plot × species matrix, partitioned into replacement (turnover) and richness-difference (nestedness) components (Legendre & De Cáceres 2013); rarefied LCBD was used to reduce sampling-intensity artifacts.

### 2.3 Remote-sensing predictors
**Static canopy structure.** Airborne LiDAR from the NEON Airborne Observation Platform was summarized into canopy structural metrics spanning height (mean and 95th-percentile canopy height, maximum height), vertical organization (foliage height diversity, vertical canopy index, vertical SD and CV), heterogeneity (Gini coefficient, rumple, rugosity, height ratio), gap structure (deep-gap fraction), and leaf area index. For the static-state analysis, each metric was represented by its multi-year mean at each plot.

**Static spectral state.** Spectral vegetation indices were computed from a single, consistent source — NEON bidirectional-reflectance (BRDF-corrected) surface reflectance (the .002 product) — as individual indices (NDVI, EVI, ARVI, SAVI). We deliberately used individual vegetation indices rather than a combined spectral-diversity metric, and excluded PRI and fPAR because of cross-year inconsistency in the provisional products.

**Structural dynamics.** Repeat LiDAR acquisitions were used to estimate the interannual trend (per-year slope) of each structural metric at each plot, over the available observation window. These trends index the *rate and direction* of canopy structural change (e.g., height and leaf-area accumulation, gap opening) independent of the mean state.

**Ecosystem productivity.** We used three independent estimates of gross primary productivity (GPP), none derived from vegetation greenness indices. Two are satellite-constrained model products extracted at plots via Google Earth Engine: MODIS MOD17 GPP and the Penman–Monteith–Leuning PML-V2 GPP (CAS/IGSNRR/PML/V2_v017), each averaged over available years. The third is directly measured eddy-covariance GPP from NEON flux towers (AmeriFlux/FLUXNET), available for 16 of the 19 sites as a site-level annual value; three sites were unavailable (one lacking a co-located tower, two lacking processed annual GPP).

### 2.4 Contextual covariates
**Disturbance.** Because remote disturbance products (MTBS, Hansen, NBR) missed insect and selective-harvest events at these sites, disturbance was defined from NEON's own event records (DP1.10111) together with in-situ plant-status observations. We characterized disturbance severity and recency, distinguished chronic "press" (e.g., insect-driven mortality) from discrete "pulse" (fire, windthrow) regimes, and, for change analyses, used a before–after/control–impact (BACI) design.

**Land-use heterogeneity.** Surrounding land-use context was summarized as fragmentation (edge density) and forest fraction within a neighborhood of each plot.

**Climate.** Site-level mean annual temperature and mean annual precipitation were obtained from NEON field-site metadata and used as continuous climate covariates in a sensitivity analysis (§2.5).

**Stand age.** Effective stand age was obtained from the GAMI global forest-age product (100 m), with a Global 30 m forest-age layer used as a complementary recency proxy (saturating at ~40 yr). Because stand age carried substantial within-site homogeneity (ICC ≈ 0.75), age effects were interpreted at the site level (effective n ≈ 19), and all age analyses were re-run with 20-year age bins (per product-developer guidance) to confirm robustness to age-estimate uncertainty.

### 2.5 Statistical analysis

All continuous predictors were standardized (z-scores) prior to analysis. Before model fitting, pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors (VIF) were examined and predictors were removed iteratively until all retained variables had VIF < 5. Because several canopy-property classes are themselves strongly cross-correlated in these forests — canopy height, for example, covaries closely with leaf area and with structural heterogeneity — this screening retained the least-redundant static metrics (rugosity, vertical CV, vertical complexity index, and leaf-area index for structure, and a single spectral index, EVI), while height and gap-fraction metrics, collinear with the retained variables, were dropped. Canopy height, although ecologically central, was strongly collinear with rugosity (r = 0.80) and correlated with leaf-area index (r = 0.58); adding it to the full model did not improve fit (ΔAIC = +1.3, ΔR² < 0.001, p = 0.41), and rugosity — which captures both vertical extent and horizontal structural heterogeneity — was retained in its place. Structural-trend metrics were screened separately: because mean canopy metrics scale together with stand development whereas their rates of change are more weakly correlated, more temporal than static predictors satisfied the criterion (seven trends retained). Both GPP products satisfied the criterion, and all predictors entering the models had VIF < 5 (maximum 4.0).

Relationships were estimated with linear models that included NEON domain as a fixed effect, separating broad biogeographic differences from within-domain ecological relationships, with standard errors clustered by site to account for the non-independence of plots within sites. Because NEON domains are explicitly defined by broad climatic and biogeographic similarity, climate and domain are strongly collinear and were not entered jointly; instead, as a sensitivity analysis (below), domain fixed effects were replaced by continuous climate covariates (mean annual temperature and precipitation from NEON site metadata) to verify that the remote-sensing contributions — in particular the diversity–productivity relationship — were not driven by climate. A site random intercept was initially specified but its variance collapsed to approximately zero after inclusion of domain fixed effects and canopy predictors, reflecting the near-singular structure of the NEON design in which several domains contain only a single site. Fixed-effect estimates were closely similar between the singular mixed model and the clustered linear model (site random-effect variance estimated at 0; mean absolute difference in standardized coefficients = 0.03); only the latter is reported because likelihood-based inference remained well defined.

Model assumptions were evaluated using residual-versus-fitted plots, quantile–quantile plots, and Breusch–Pagan tests for heteroscedasticity. Residuals were heteroscedastic and right-skewed on the raw scale; log-transforming the strictly positive Hill numbers restored homoscedasticity and approximate normality (Breusch–Pagan p > 0.4, Shapiro–Wilk p > 0.2), whereas LCBD indices remained skewed under transformation and were therefore analyzed with the heteroscedasticity-consistent, site-clustered standard errors described above. Because LCBD indices are bounded proportions, the nested comparisons were additionally repeated with beta regression: conclusions were identical for turnover and for the structural-dynamics increment throughout, and the only difference was that the marginal productivity increment for nestedness, non-significant under the Gaussian model (p = 0.09), reached significance under beta regression (p = 0.03); the Gaussian result is reported as the conservative choice (Supplementary Table S2). All nested-model conclusions were also identical on the raw and log scales (Supplementary Information). Residual spatial autocorrelation was evaluated using Moran's I based on k-nearest-neighbour (k = 8) spatial weights with a permutation test; where significant residual dependence remained, models including a smooth spatial trend (a second-order polynomial in longitude and latitude) were fitted as sensitivity analyses to confirm that ecological inference was unchanged. For families of interaction tests, false-discovery-rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied.

**Overall analytical framework.** Rather than comparing remote-sensing products independently, we evaluated remote sensing as a hierarchy of ecological information, asking whether static canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity each contributed complementary information about tree diversity beyond information already contained in preceding model components. Analyses followed a nested hierarchical framework fitted over a common sample:

- **M0 (baseline):** NEON domain only.
- **M1 (State):** M0 + static canopy structure + static spectral properties.
- **M2 (State + Dynamics):** M1 + structural trends derived from repeated LiDAR observations.
- **M3 (State + Dynamics + Function):** M2 + independent GPP.

Incremental explanatory value was quantified as increases in partial R² (ΔR²). Because inference rested on only 19 site clusters — a regime in which asymptotic cluster-robust standard errors are anticonservative — block-level significance was assessed primarily with restricted wild cluster bootstrap tests (Rademacher weights, 999 replicates) of the null that the added block's coefficients were jointly zero; asymptotic cluster-robust joint Wald (F) tests, likelihood-ratio tests, and AIC are reported as secondary summaries. Because sequential partial R² (the increment from adding a block) and blockwise semi-partial R² (a block's unique contribution in the fully adjusted model) are distinct quantities, both are reported and labelled explicitly. To quantify complementarity among remote-sensing dimensions, semi-partial R² values were calculated for each predictor block (static structure, spectral properties, structural dynamics, and productivity), representing the variance uniquely explained by each block after accounting for all remaining predictors. Interpretation focuses primarily on these unique contributions (semi-partial R²); shared explanatory variance among predictor blocks was additionally explored using commonality analysis and is presented in the Supplementary Information.

**O1 — Static canopy state.** Within M1 we compared the unique contributions and standardized coefficients of structural versus spectral predictors for each diversity component, testing the prediction that spectral information is more strongly associated with alpha diversity whereas structural attributes contribute relatively more information about beta (turnover) diversity.

**O2 — Structural dynamics.** The incremental contribution of structural dynamics was evaluated through nested-model comparisons between M1 and M2 using ΔR², ΔAIC, and likelihood-ratio tests. To investigate ecological controls on canopy dynamics, each structural trend was subsequently modeled as a function of stand age and disturbance regime while controlling for domain and forest type. Partial regression was used to isolate stand-age effects from broad geographic variation, and only relationships remaining significant after this adjustment were interpreted. Apparent relationships between initial canopy structure and subsequent structural change were evaluated against a regression-to-the-mean (RTM) null model that assumed no biological feedback between initial structure and subsequent change while preserving the observed variance of repeated measurements; only observed relationships significantly exceeding RTM expectations were interpreted as evidence of biological regulation. Full details of the RTM null-model simulation are provided in Supplementary Methods S1.

**O3 — Ecosystem productivity.** Independent GPP products were introduced only after accounting for static canopy state and structural dynamics, allowing assessment of whether realized ecosystem carbon uptake contributed information beyond canopy properties rather than serving as an alternative representation of canopy greenness. Both linear and quadratic terms were fitted to characterize productivity–diversity relationships and distinguish monotonic species–energy patterns from nonlinear responses. Plot-level MODIS and PML GPP products were analyzed at plot resolution and additionally aggregated to site means for comparison with site-level eddy-covariance tower GPP. Tower-derived GPP analyses were restricted to the 16 sites with available flux observations, and no imputation was performed for sites lacking tower measurements.

**O4 — Context dependence.** Ecological context was evaluated through interaction terms between each remote-sensing dimension (state, dynamics, and productivity) and (i) disturbance severity and recency, (ii) surrounding land-use heterogeneity, and (iii) stand age. Interactions were organized by the conceptual framework (State × Context, Dynamics × Context, Function × Context) and, to remain consistent with the nested models, used the same retained predictors (the four structural metrics, EVI, and the two GPP products), each crossed with the context variables; across the three context families this comprised 168 interaction terms, fitted with site-clustered standard errors, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied within each family. This screen was treated as exploratory except for the two families in which effects were coherent (productivity × stand age; structure × disturbance), which we probed with simple slopes at ±1 SD of the moderator. Disturbance effects on composition were additionally evaluated with a before–after/control–impact (BACI) design, in which the impact was the treatment × post-disturbance interaction; press and pulse disturbance regimes were, separately, compared descriptively for their pre-to-post structural change.

**Sensitivity analyses.** Robustness was assessed by (i) repeating stand-age analyses with 20-year age classes following the GAMI forest-age product developers' recommendation; (ii) repeating age analyses with the alternative Global 30 m forest-age product; (iii) restricting tower-GPP analyses to the 16 sites with available flux observations; (iv) evaluating residual spatial autocorrelation with Moran's I and, where significant, fitting models with a smooth spatial trend to confirm that conclusions regarding the incremental contributions of canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity were unchanged; and (v) replacing NEON domain fixed effects with continuous climate covariates (NEON site mean annual temperature and precipitation) to confirm that the remote-sensing increments — and in particular the diversity–productivity relationship, for which GPP retained a positive, site-clustered association with alpha diversity beyond climate (MODIS β = +0.20, p = 0.02) — persisted under explicit climate control.

All analyses were implemented in Python 3.13 (pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn) together with Google Earth Engine for extraction of GPP and land-cover products. This hierarchical framework quantifies the incremental ecological information contained in canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity, allowing the complementary contributions of multiple remote-sensing dimensions to forest biodiversity to be evaluated within a single analytical framework. ## 3. Results

### 3.1 State, dynamics, and productivity carry complementary biodiversity information
Biogeographic structure alone (NEON domain) accounted for a large share of compositional turnover (LCBD turnover R² = 0.51) but less of local diversity and richness difference (Hill q1 R² = 0.40, Hill q2 = 0.36, LCBD nestedness = 0.17). Beyond this baseline, the three remote-sensing dimensions together explained a further 21% of alpha-diversity variance (Hill q1 and q2), 8% of turnover, and 15% of nestedness (Fig. 1a). Each successive model block increased explanatory power, but formal support for the increments was uneven once inference accounted for the small number of site clusters. Structural dynamics increased sequential partial R² by 0.072, 0.073, 0.026, and 0.072 for Hill q1, Hill q2, turnover, and nestedness, respectively; under wild cluster bootstrap tests the dynamics increment was marginal for the two alpha metrics (p = 0.06 for both) and not significant for turnover (p = 0.76) or nestedness (p = 0.24). Adding productivity increased partial R² by 0.008, 0.008, 0.029, and 0.008, and the only block increment that remained significant under the wild bootstrap was productivity for compositional turnover (p = 0.003); its increments for the alpha metrics and nestedness were not (p = 0.11–0.17). Asymptotic cluster-robust Wald, likelihood-ratio, and AIC criteria agreed in direction but, as expected with 19 clusters, were substantially more liberal (Supplementary Table S1). The incremental tests are therefore conservative, and we lean on the variance partition below — a descriptive decomposition insensitive to the clustering problem — to characterize the division of information among dimensions.

Partitioning the explained variance into unique block contributions (semi-partial R²; Fig. 1b) showed that each remote-sensing dimension retained information after mutual adjustment, with shared fractions accounting for only 8–14% of the total variance attributable to remote-sensing predictors. The dominant information source differed among diversity components. Static structure carried the largest unique share of alpha-diversity variance (Hill q1 = 0.106; Hill q2 = 0.105), productivity formed the largest unique block for compositional turnover (0.029), and structural dynamics formed the largest unique block for richness difference (nestedness = 0.071); structural dynamics also made the second-largest contribution to alpha diversity. Spectral greenness contributed a smaller but independent alpha-diversity signal (0.010–0.013) and little unique beta-diversity information (turnover = 0.005; nestedness = 0.001). Residual spatial autocorrelation was statistically detectable but weak in magnitude (Moran's I = 0.05–0.15), and block-level estimates were stable after inclusion of a smooth spatial trend. The direction and overall interpretation of the results were also robust to alternative response transformations, beta-regression models for the bounded LCBD indices, and continuous-climate baselines, although the significance of individual marginal effects varied in some sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S5).

### 3.2 Static canopy structure provides the broadest biodiversity signal
Canopy structure was the dominant static predictor of local diversity. In fully standardized models, the vertical complexity index had the largest effect on both Hill q1 (β = +0.37, p = 3 × 10⁻⁶) and Hill q2 (β = +0.37, p = 4 × 10⁻⁶), followed by leaf-area index (β = +0.27, p = 0.004–0.008). Spectral greenness (EVI) was a significant but smaller positive predictor of alpha diversity (β = +0.24, p = 0.01), and its unique contribution (semi-partial R² ≈ 0.01) was an order of magnitude below that of structure (§3.1). Structure was also the only static dimension with statistically supported associations with beta diversity: compositional turnover loaded on the vertical complexity index (β = +0.15, p = 0.03) but not on EVI (β = +0.17, p = 0.34), and richness difference (nestedness) loaded on vertical CV (β = −0.16, p = 0.007) but not on EVI (p = 0.61). Spectral greenness therefore provided a modest alpha-specific contribution, whereas canopy structure supplied a broader signal encompassing both local diversity and compositional differentiation.

### 3.3 Structural dynamics add information and vary along successional and disturbance gradients
Interannual structural change increased explanatory power beyond the static state, although under few-cluster-valid tests this increment was only marginal for local diversity and not significant for the beta components (§3.1). Descriptively, however, structural dynamics formed the largest unique block for richness difference (nestedness, semi-partial R² = 0.071) and the second-largest for alpha diversity, and the trends themselves varied systematically with stand age and disturbance context, indicating that at least part of the temporal signal was ecologically structured rather than randomly distributed across plots. Along the successional axis, the rate of vertical-complexity and leaf-area accumulation declined with stand age (VCI trend vs age β = −0.25, p = 9 × 10⁻⁴; LAI trend β = −0.22, p = 0.003), while canopy-gap dynamics increased in older stands (deep-gap trend β = +0.28, p = 2 × 10⁻⁴), a pattern consistent with a shift from rapid stand aggradation in younger forests toward greater gap formation in older stands. Along the disturbance axis, disturbed plots showed contrasting structural change (post- minus pre-disturbance LAI): plots in chronic, insect-driven ("press") regimes had a negative median change whereas plots affected by discrete ("pulse") events showed net recovery (median change −0.18 vs +0.22). Because disturbance regime varied within sites and few sites could be cleanly assigned to a single regime, this contrast is reported as a descriptive, plot-level pattern rather than a formal test. Apparent relationships between initial canopy structure and subsequent change were largely consistent with a regression-to-the-mean null once tested explicitly and were interpreted conservatively.

### 3.4 Productivity adds limited, product-dependent information beyond canopy properties
In single-product models, plot-level alpha diversity increased with GPP (b = +0.52 per 1-SD increase in MODIS GPP, p = 4 × 10⁻¹¹; b = +0.34 per 1-SD PML-V2 GPP, p = 3 × 10⁻⁵; log-diversity response), with no evidence of a unimodal, hump-shaped relationship (Fig. 4), whereas the site-level eddy-covariance tower association was positive but imprecisely estimated (r = +0.21, n = 16, not significant). This association was not fully explained by broad climate: when NEON domain was replaced by continuous mean annual temperature and precipitation, GPP retained a positive, site-clustered association with alpha diversity beyond climate (MODIS b = +0.20, p = 0.02 for Hill q1). After canopy state and dynamics were accounted for, however, productivity's unique contribution to local diversity was not significant under the wild bootstrap (§3.1); the only productivity increment that survived that test was for compositional turnover (p = 0.003). This turnover signal was carried almost entirely by one product: on its own, PML-V2 GPP was weakly positively associated with turnover (b = +0.07, p = 0.04) whereas MODIS GPP showed no association (b = −0.01, p = 0.70), and in the joint model their coefficients opposed one another (PML b = +0.08, MODIS b = −0.05) with the two products correlated at r = 0.75. The productivity block therefore contained independent information about turnover, but the opposing product-specific coefficients preclude a directional ecological interpretation and may reflect differences between the GPP products or suppression arising from their shared variance (Supplementary Table S7). We accordingly do not read the productivity–turnover association as a simple local species–energy mechanism.

### 3.5 Ecological context modifies remote sensing–diversity associations
We then examined how the framework predictors interacted with ecological context. Because the full screen comprised 168 interaction terms across three context families (fitted with site-clustered errors and Benjamini–Hochberg corrected within family; 11 survived, Supplementary Table S6), we treat the two coherent patterns below as the interpretable results and the remainder as exploratory. First, the productivity–diversity association strengthened with stand age: GPP × stand-age interactions were positive for both alpha metrics (q ≤ 0.02), and simple slopes confirmed that the GPP–diversity relationship was essentially flat in the youngest stands (b = +0.06, p = 0.64) and positive only in older stands (b = +0.39, p = 0.004). Second, the structure–diversity association weakened with disturbance: interactions of vertical complexity and leaf-area index with disturbance severity and recency were negative for alpha diversity (q ≤ 0.03); the VCI–diversity slope remained positive at all disturbance levels but declined from b = +0.48 (p < 0.001) in the least-disturbed plots to b = +0.22 (p = 0.003) in the most-disturbed. Among land-use interactions only one survived correction (EVI × forest fraction on turnover, q < 0.01), reported as exploratory. Direct land-use effects were clearer: greater edge density was associated with lower diversity (b = −0.30, p = 4 × 10⁻⁴) and greater forest cover with higher diversity (b = +0.22, p = 0.002). Finally, in the before–after/control–impact analysis, the treatment × post-disturbance interaction estimated a decline of approximately one species (b = −1.07), although the evidence was borderline (p = 0.05), whereas abundance-weighted (Hill) diversity was unchanged (p = 0.63) — consistent with losses concentrated among species contributing little to dominance.
