### 3.1 Canopy state and structural dynamics carry complementary plot-level biodiversity information

The plot-level framework comprises two remote-sensing dimensions observed at the plot grain — static canopy state (structure + spectral) and structural dynamics (multitemporal LiDAR trends). Ecosystem productivity is analysed separately at the site level (§3.4), because the satellite GPP products are 500 m and carry essentially no plot-scale variation (intra-class correlation ≈ 0.92, i.e. ~92% of plot-level GPP variance is between-site), and their plot-level unique contribution did not replicate across PML-V2 versions (significant with v017/2000–2020 but not with the latest V2.2a/2000–2024).

Biogeographic structure alone (NEON domain) accounted for a large share of compositional turnover (LCBD turnover R² = 0.58) and between a third and a half of the remaining components (Hill q1 R² = 0.49, Hill q2 = 0.45, LCBD nestedness = 0.33). Beyond this baseline, canopy state plus structural dynamics together explained a further 15% of alpha-diversity variance (Hill q1 and q2), but only 2% of compositional turnover and 9% of richness difference (nestedness) (Fig. 1a).

Structural dynamics increased sequential partial R² by 0.042, 0.039, 0.013, and 0.056 for Hill q1, Hill q2, turnover, and nestedness beyond canopy state; under restricted wild cluster bootstrap tests (999 replicates; rarefied LCBD averaged over 999 Monte-Carlo draws) this increment was significant for both alpha metrics (p = 0.014 and 0.007) but not for turnover (p = 0.77) or nestedness (p = 0.14). Asymptotic cluster-robust Wald criteria agreed in direction but, as expected with few clusters, were more liberal (Supplementary Table S1).

Partitioning the explained variance into unique block contributions (semi-partial R²; Fig. 1b) showed that each remote-sensing dimension retained information after mutual adjustment. The dominant information source differed among diversity components. **Static structure carried the largest unique share of alpha diversity** (Hill q1 = 0.079; Hill q2 = 0.072), whereas **structural dynamics formed the largest unique block for richness difference** (nestedness = 0.057) and the second-largest for alpha diversity. For compositional turnover the unique fractions were all small (≤ 0.014) and the shared fraction was correspondingly large (≈ 29% of the turnover variance attributable to remote sensing, versus 11–13% for the other components), indicating that turnover carries little uniquely remotely-sensed information; productivity was nonetheless the only block whose turnover increment was statistically supported (above). Spectral greenness contributed a small but independent alpha-diversity signal (0.012–0.013) and essentially no unique beta-diversity information (turnover = 0.009; nestedness = 0.001). Residual spatial autocorrelation was statistically detectable but modest (Moran's I = 0.09 for nestedness to 0.22 for turnover; all p = 0.001), and block-level estimates were stable after inclusion of a smooth spatial trend.

### 3.2 Static canopy structure provides the broadest biodiversity signal

Canopy structure was the dominant static predictor of local diversity. In fully standardized models, the vertical complexity index (VCI) had the largest effect on both Hill q1 (β = +0.30, p = 1 × 10⁻⁷) and Hill q2 (β = +0.28, p = 1 × 10⁻⁶), followed by leaf-area index (β = +0.26, p = 0.003 for q1; β = +0.23, p = 0.014 for q2). Spectral greenness (EVI) was a significant but smaller positive predictor of alpha diversity (β = +0.22, p = 9 × 10⁻⁵ for q1; β = +0.24, p = 8 × 10⁻⁵ for q2), and its unique contribution (semi-partial R² ≈ 0.012) was roughly six-fold below that of structure (§3.1).

Structure was also the only static dimension with a supported association with beta diversity, and only for richness difference: nestedness loaded negatively on vertical CV (β = −0.15, p = 0.008), whereas **no static predictor was significantly associated with compositional turnover** (VCI β = +0.07, p = 0.22; EVI β = +0.07, p = 0.57). Spectral greenness therefore provided a modest, alpha-specific contribution and no unique beta signal, whereas canopy structure supplied a broader signal spanning local diversity and richness difference. (Note: unlike the 19-site analysis, turnover is no longer significantly related to VCI; the turnover signal is weak throughout.)

### 3.3 Structural dynamics add information and vary along successional and disturbance gradients

Interannual structural change increased explanatory power beyond the static state, significantly so for local diversity and, descriptively, forming the largest unique block for richness difference (nestedness, semi-partial R² = 0.057; §3.1). The trends carrying this signal were interpretable: nestedness loaded on the leaf-area trend (β = −0.25, p = 2 × 10⁻⁵) and the height-ratio trend (β = −0.29, p = 0.003), and alpha diversity on the foliage-height-diversity trend (β = −0.12, p = 0.003) and rumple trend (β = +0.08, p = 0.04), indicating that at least part of the temporal signal was ecologically structured rather than noise.

Along the successional axis (stand age from the GAMI v3.1 100 m product, re-extracted for all 26 sites; ensemble-mean 2020, n = 639 aged plots, median 95 yr, range 7–204), the rate of leaf-area accumulation declined with stand age (LAI-trend vs age β = −0.15, p = 0.03), consistent with faster canopy development in younger stands. The diversity–age relationship itself was weakly hump-shaped (Hill q1 quadratic p = 0.02); we treat this as a biogeographic age confound (older stands concentrated in particular domains) rather than a causal successional effect, consistent with the framing adopted throughout. Along the disturbance axis, structural change (post- minus pre-disturbance LAI) was compared between chronic ("press": insect/mortality) and discrete ("pulse": fire/wind/flood/harvest) regimes at the site level, with the regime classification derived from NEON in-situ stem-status records (vst plantStatus) and documented site-event reports (DP1.10111), both re-extracted for all 26 sites. Neither regime showed strong net change: press sites had a slight median LAI decline (−0.029, 23 sites) and pulse sites essentially none (~0.000, 18 sites; sites can contribute plots to both regimes), and the two did not differ detectably (Mann–Whitney not computable at the site level). We therefore report no robust press/pulse structural contrast. This is a fragile descriptive result: it does not survive the expansion to 26 sites (the modest pulse-recovery signal seen at 19 sites disappears), and the regime label is itself classification-dependent — e.g. Yellowstone (YELL) reads as fire/pulse in the MTBS remote record but as press in the NEON in-situ record, because the "burned" stem-status code is rarely applied. Apparent relationships between initial structure and subsequent change were largely consistent with a regression-to-the-mean null and interpreted conservatively. ### 3.4 Productivity relates to diversity at the site scale (species–energy)

We analyse ecosystem productivity at the site level rather than as a plot-level framework block, for two reasons established above: (i) the satellite GPP products are 500 m and carry almost no plot-scale variation (ICC ≈ 0.92; within-site CV ≈ 9–16%), so a "plot-level" GPP is effectively a site value replicated across plots; and (ii) the plot-level unique GPP contribution was not robust — significant with PML-V2 v017 (2000–2020) but non-significant with the latest V2.2a (2000–2024; function block ΔR² = 0.003–0.012, all p > 0.18), and the two satellite products (MODIS, PML) took opposing signs for turnover despite r = 0.85 (suppression), precluding a directional plot-level interpretation.

At the site level the species–energy relationship is clear and robust. The gold-standard eddy-covariance tower GPP was positively and monotonically related to site-mean alpha diversity across the 22 tower sites (Hill q1: r = +0.59, p = 0.004; Hill q2: r = +0.57, p = 0.006; quadratic term n.s., p = 0.87 — no hump), a signal much clearer than in the 19-site sample (previously r = +0.21, n.s.) because the boreal-Alaska towers extended the gradient to its low end (~380–2350 gC m⁻² yr⁻¹). The satellite products, aggregated to site means, agreed: PML-V2 tracked the tower closely (r = +0.87 site-level; Spearman 0.83) and MODIS somewhat less (r = +0.85, Spearman 0.64), and both showed the same monotonic, hump-free increase. GPP also exceeded a pure climate signal (GPP retained a positive association with alpha diversity beyond mean annual temperature and precipitation). We therefore report productivity as a robust, site-scale species–energy relationship, distinct from — and not competing at plot scale with — the canopy-structure signal that dominates local diversity.

After canopy state and dynamics were accounted for, productivity retained a significant increment for alpha diversity and for compositional turnover under the wild bootstrap (§3.1). The turnover signal, however, was product-dependent and consistent with statistical suppression: in the full model MODIS and PML GPP took strongly opposing signs (MODIS β = −0.24, p = 0.04; PML β = +0.36, p = 0.02) despite being correlated at r = 0.85, and on their own neither was clearly associated with turnover (MODIS β = −0.01, p = 0.83; PML β = +0.06, p = 0.13). The productivity block therefore contained independent information about turnover, but the opposing product-specific coefficients preclude a directional ecological interpretation and likely reflect differences between the GPP products or suppression arising from their shared variance (Supplementary Table S7). We accordingly do not read the productivity–turnover association as a simple local species–energy mechanism.

### 3.5 Ecological context modifies remote sensing–diversity associations

We then examined how the framework predictors interacted with ecological context. Across three context families (stand age, disturbance, land use) the interaction screen (fitted with site-clustered errors, Benjamini–Hochberg corrected within family) identified twelve significant terms; we treat the two coherent patterns below as interpretable and the remainder as exploratory.

First, the productivity–diversity association was **descriptively steeper in older stands** — simple slopes of MODIS GPP on Hill q1 rose from b = +0.17 (p = 0.13) at −1 SD stand age to +0.27 (p = 0.01) at the mean and +0.38 (p = 0.007) at +1 SD — **but the formal GPP × stand-age interaction did not survive FDR correction at 26 sites** (MODIS q = 0.26, PML q = 0.35). This is a downgrade from the 19-site analysis (where the interaction was FDR-significant, q ≤ 0.02); adding the boreal-Alaska age range weakened the moderation, so we now report age-dependence of the productivity signal as *suggestive rather than statistically supported*. Second, the **structure–diversity association weakened with disturbance** (disturbance now characterized for all 26 sites from NBR, Hansen forest-loss, and MTBS fire records): the leaf-area-index × severity interaction was negative for alpha diversity (q = 0.004), and disturbance recency additionally moderated the productivity signal (MODIS GPP × recency, q = 0.005). The VCI–diversity simple slope remained positive at all disturbance levels but declined from b = +0.39 (p = 9 × 10⁻⁷) in the least-disturbed plots to b = +0.21 (p = 1 × 10⁻⁴) in the most-disturbed (the VCI × severity interaction itself was marginal at 26 sites, q = 0.08, the FDR-surviving structural term being LAI). Among land-use interactions (land cover re-extracted for all 26 sites from ESA WorldCover), spectral-by-land-use terms on turnover survived correction (EVI × forest fraction, q = 4 × 10⁻⁵; EVI × edge density, q = 0.002; EVI × land-cover diversity, q = 0.009) and are reported as exploratory. Direct land-use effects, by contrast, were weak and non-significant at 26 sites (edge density on log-diversity β = −0.09, p = 0.10; forest fraction β = +0.06, p = 0.22), a downgrade from the 19-site analysis (edge β ≈ −0.30) that reflects the added heterogeneity of the boreal and southeastern sites [exact original model spec to reconcile]. Finally, in the before–after/control–impact analysis (26 sites), the treatment × post-disturbance interaction estimated a decline of approximately one species (ΔRichness β = −1.05, p = 0.069; n = 79 impact, 215 control plots), whereas abundance-weighted (Hill) diversity was unchanged (p = 0.81), consistent with losses concentrated among species contributing little to dominance.
