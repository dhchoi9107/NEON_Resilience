# 4. Discussion — 26-site + TREE-recovered draft

> Grounded in the 26-site analysis (n = 644 alpha / 579 beta plots, 12 NEON domains
> from the conterminous US to interior Alaska). Numbers cross-referenced to
> `results/O0_framework/*.csv` and the O2–O4 26-site outputs. Companion to
> RESULTS_26site_draft.md and METHODS_26site_draft.md.

Across temperate and boreal forests of North America, the three remote-sensing dimensions we distinguished — static canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity — carried **complementary rather than redundant** information about tree diversity, and, critically, the dominant signal differed among diversity components. Static canopy structure held the largest unique share of local (alpha) diversity, structural dynamics the largest unique share of richness difference (nestedness), and productivity was the only dimension whose contribution to compositional turnover was statistically supported. This division of labour among sensors and diversity dimensions is the central result, and it was robust to a near-continental expansion of the sampling frame and to the recovery of a previously data-limited site. Below we interpret each dimension, then turn to two findings that only the expanded, honestly re-analysed dataset revealed: the *strengthening* of the productivity–diversity signal against the eddy-covariance gold standard, and the *failure to replicate* a stand-age moderation that appeared significant in the smaller sample.

## 4.1 Canopy structure is the broadest window on local diversity (O1)

Canopy structure was the dominant static predictor of alpha diversity (vertical complexity index β ≈ +0.30, leaf-area index β ≈ +0.25), and it was the only static dimension with a supported association with any beta component (vertical CV with nestedness). This is consistent with the long-standing view that three-dimensional canopy architecture indexes the physical niche space over which tree species partition light and growing space (MacArthur & MacArthur 1961; Davies & Asner 2014): where the canopy is vertically more complex and leaf area higher, more species coexist locally. Spectral greenness (EVI) added a smaller, strictly alpha-specific signal (unique R² ≈ 0.012) and no unique beta information. The implication for remote biodiversity monitoring is direct: **structural and spectral observations are not interchangeable**. Spectral indices, still the most common remote proxy for diversity, capture a real but narrow slice of local richness and are essentially silent on how communities differ across space; LiDAR structure captures both. Notably, in the 26-site sample the earlier association between structure and compositional turnover weakened to non-significance, sharpening rather than blurring this picture: static structure informs *how much* diversity a stand holds far more than *how* its composition differs from its neighbours.

## 4.2 Structural dynamics add information about which species are missing (O2)

Repeated LiDAR observations contributed information beyond static state, and did so most distinctly for the richness-difference (nestedness) component, for which interannual trends formed the largest unique block (semi-partial R² = 0.057). Ecologically, this suggests that the *trajectory* of canopy development — not just its current configuration — carries a signature of which species a stand has gained or lost relative to richer neighbours. The trends were not noise: leaf-area accumulation slowed significantly with stand age (LAI-trend vs GAMI age β = −0.15), the expected succession signature of rapid aggradation in young stands giving way to slower change in mature forest. The inference for this link deserves a word on method. The joint *block-increment* test — asking whether the entire dynamics block raises R² beyond chance — did not reach significance under the wild cluster bootstrap; but that test is a conservative, low-power procedure for a network with only ~12 domains, and it answers a different question from "which trends matter." When we estimate the individual coefficients in a Bayesian multilevel model that propagates the site- and domain-level uncertainty directly (0 divergences, R̂ ≤ 1.002), three structural-dynamics trends are **credibly** associated with nestedness — the canopy height-ratio trend (β = −0.31, 95% HDI [−0.42, −0.20]), the vertical-CV trend (+0.20 [+0.08, +0.32]) and the leaf-area trend (−0.19 [−0.31, −0.07]) — the largest credible block for this component. We therefore treat the dynamics → nestedness relationship not as a merely descriptive pattern but as a **coefficient-level supported result**, with the caveat that the conservative joint block test is underpowered at this network size; the honest reading is that the effect is real in these data and awaits a stiffer joint test in a network with more independent sites. That the signal is strongest for nestedness rather than turnover is itself informative, and it cuts against the intuition the terms invite: although "turnover" *sounds* like the dynamic component and "nestedness" like a static plot-to-plot difference, the remote-sensing signals map by the **nature of the underlying process, not by the connotation of the label** — lateral species replacement (turnover), a fundamentally spatial contrast between similarly-developed stands, is tracked by static structural *dissimilarity* (the variation-hypothesis result, §3.1b), whereas directional richness loss/gain (nestedness) is tracked by the *temporal trend*, because a stand shedding or accruing species relative to richer neighbours is on a developmental trajectory that its canopy dynamics record.

## 4.3 Realized productivity supports a monotonic species–energy relationship (O3)

Because we deliberately avoided greenness as a productivity proxy, the productivity results speak to energy availability rather than canopy density. All three independent GPP estimates — MODIS MOD17, PML-V2, and eddy-covariance tower GPP — showed positive species–energy relationships, and the **gold-standard tower relationship strengthened markedly in the expanded sample** (Hill q1 vs tower GPP r = +0.59, p = 0.004, n = 22), precisely because the boreal-Alaska towers extended the productivity gradient to its low end (~380 gC m⁻² yr⁻¹, roughly a quarter of the most productive temperate sites). The relationship was monotonic, with no evidence of a unimodal hump in either MODIS or the tower data — reinforcing our decision to treat earlier greenness-based "productivity humps" as artifacts of canopy density rather than energy. After canopy state and dynamics were accounted for, productivity's unique contribution was modest and, for turnover, product-dependent: MODIS and PML GPP took opposing signs in the joint model despite being correlated at r = 0.85, a classic suppression signature that precludes a directional reading. We therefore frame productivity as a genuine but secondary and partially collinear source of diversity information — valuable chiefly because it corroborates a monotonic species–energy pattern with data that do not depend on the canopy structure already in the model.

## 4.4 Context dependence, and a cautionary tale about small networks (O4)

The expansion delivered its sharpest methodological lesson here. In the 19-site sample, the productivity–diversity relationship appeared to strengthen significantly with stand age — an attractive successional story. With true continuous stand age (GAMI v3.1) re-extracted for all 26 sites, **that interaction no longer survived multiple-testing correction** (MODIS × age q = 0.26), even though the simple slopes still trend in the same direction. The moderation was, in effect, a small-sample result that did not replicate once the boreal age range was added. We report it as suggestive only, and we flag it as a general caution: **context-moderation effects estimated from a few dozen site clusters are fragile**, and networks like NEON are near the lower limit of what few-cluster-valid inference can support for interaction tests. By contrast, the structure–disturbance interaction was reproducible in direction — the structure–diversity slope declined monotonically from least- to most-disturbed plots — although the specific structural metric carrying it shifted (from vertical complexity to leaf area) and the before–after/control–impact richness loss became borderline (≈ one species, p = 0.069). Disturbance appears to genuinely erode the structure–diversity coupling, with losses concentrated among rare species (abundance-weighted diversity unchanged), but the effect sizes are small and the disturbance sample modest.

## 4.5 Limitations

Three limitations bound our inference. First, **statistical power at the site level remains the central constraint**: even at 26 sites, several NEON domains contain a single site, so the wild-bootstrap *joint block* tests are conservative for beta components and for interactions, and some effects that are credible at the coefficient level (dynamics → nestedness, supported by the Bayesian multilevel model) or descriptively large (disturbance moderation) do not clear the joint few-cluster increment test. Second, **covariate provenance is heterogeneous**: two sites (TREE) required recovery from a shared AOP flight box, one (ORNL) lacks the bidirectional spectral product entirely, four lack processed tower GPP, and the GAMI age and land-cover products were re-extracted from newer versions than any prior analysis; we validated the age re-extraction against the original (r = 0.83) and re-extracted all 26 sites from single sources to guarantee internal consistency, but cross-version comparability with earlier literature should be treated with care. Third, **turnover carries little uniquely remote-sensed information** in this system (shared variance ≈ 29%), so our strongest beta claims concern richness difference rather than replacement, and the productivity–turnover signal in particular rests on a suppression pattern we decline to interpret directionally.

## 4.6 Implications for remote biodiversity monitoring

Three practical conclusions follow. (i) **Match the sensor to the diversity dimension**: LiDAR structure for local richness and richness difference, independent GPP for the species–energy gradient; spectral greenness alone is the weakest of the three and informative only for alpha. (ii) **Use realized productivity, not greenness**, when testing energy–diversity theory — the monotonic, no-hump relationship emerges cleanly only with GPP products independent of canopy density, and is best resolved across broad productivity gradients such as the temperate-to-boreal transition. (iii) **Treat context-moderation from sparse networks with scepticism**: our own headline age-moderation did not replicate when the network grew, a reminder that interaction effects are the first casualties of few-cluster inference and should be validated against expanded samples before they enter the ecological narrative.

# 5. Conclusions

Remote sensing does not observe forest biodiversity through a single window. Static canopy structure, multitemporal structural dynamics, and independently estimated ecosystem productivity encode **distinct and complementary** dimensions of tree diversity across the temperate-to-boreal forests of North America: structure carries the broadest signal and dominates local (alpha) diversity, structural dynamics contribute most uniquely to richness difference, and realized productivity supports a monotonic species–energy relationship that is corroborated by eddy-covariance measurements and is not reducible to canopy greenness. Expanding the analysis from 19 to 26 sites — and recovering a site whose airborne data were hidden under a neighbouring flight box — strengthened the core, structure- and productivity-centred conclusions while dissolving a stand-age moderation that had appeared significant in the smaller sample. The division of biodiversity information among remote-sensing dimensions is therefore real and transferable; the context-dependence of those relationships is more fragile than sparse networks can yet resolve. For biodiversity monitoring, the practical message is to combine complementary sensors deliberately, to ground productivity–diversity inference in realized carbon uptake rather than greenness, and to hold context-moderation claims to the standard of replication across expanded observational networks.
