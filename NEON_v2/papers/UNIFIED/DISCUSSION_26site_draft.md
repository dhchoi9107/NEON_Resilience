# 4. Discussion — 26-site + TREE-recovered draft

> Grounded in the 26-site analysis (n = 644 alpha / 579 beta plots, 12 NEON domains
> from the conterminous US to interior Alaska). Numbers cross-referenced to
> `results/O0_framework/*.csv` and the O2–O4 26-site outputs. Companion to
> RESULTS_26site_draft.md and METHODS_26site_draft.md.

Across temperate and boreal forests of North America, the remote-sensing dimensions we distinguished — static canopy state, structural dynamics, and ecosystem productivity — carried **complementary rather than redundant** information about tree diversity, and, critically, the dominant signal differed among diversity components. Static canopy structure held the largest unique share of local (alpha) diversity; structural dynamics the largest unique share of richness difference (nestedness); compositional turnover was tracked not by any predictor block but by remote-sensing *heterogeneity* — plots more dissimilar in structure or spectra were more dissimilar in composition; and realized productivity supported a monotonic species–energy relationship at the site scale. This division of labour — different sensors and analytical framings for different diversity components — is the central result. Two features sharpen it. First, the signals map to the *ecological process* underlying each diversity component rather than to the connotations of their names: lateral replacement (turnover) is read from static dissimilarity, directional richness loss/gain (nestedness) from temporal change. Second, the strongest practical message is a negative one about the field's most common proxy — spectral greenness was the weakest of the three dimensions, informative for alpha alone. Below we interpret each dimension in turn, then consider the context-dependence of these relationships and the limits that a continental but site-sparse network places on it.

## 4.1 Canopy structure is the broadest window on local diversity (O1)

Canopy structure was the dominant static predictor of alpha diversity (vertical complexity index β ≈ +0.30, leaf-area index β ≈ +0.25), and it was the only static dimension with a supported association with any beta component (vertical CV with nestedness). This is consistent with the long-standing view that three-dimensional canopy architecture indexes the physical niche space over which tree species partition light and growing space (MacArthur & MacArthur 1961; Davies & Asner 2014): where the canopy is vertically more complex and leaf area higher, more species coexist locally. Spectral greenness (EVI) added a smaller, strictly alpha-specific signal (unique R² ≈ 0.012) and no unique beta information. The implication for remote biodiversity monitoring is direct: **structural and spectral observations are not interchangeable**. Spectral indices, still the most common remote proxy for diversity, capture a real but narrow slice of local richness and are essentially silent on how communities differ across space; LiDAR structure captures both. Notably, no static structural predictor was significantly associated with compositional turnover as a predictor level, which sharpens rather than blurs this picture: static structure informs *how much* diversity a stand holds far more than *how* its composition differs from its neighbours — the turnover signal emerges only when structure is analysed as between-plot dissimilarity (the variation-hypothesis result, Results §3.1b; interpreted in §4.3).

## 4.2 Structural dynamics add information about which species are missing (O2)

Repeated LiDAR observations contributed information beyond static state, and did so most distinctly for the richness-difference (nestedness) component, for which interannual trends formed the largest unique block (semi-partial R² = 0.057). Ecologically, this suggests that the *trajectory* of canopy development — not just its current configuration — carries a signature of which species a stand has gained or lost relative to richer neighbours. The trends were not noise: leaf-area accumulation slowed significantly with stand age (LAI-trend vs GAMI age β = −0.15), the expected succession signature of rapid aggradation in young stands giving way to slower change in mature forest. The inference for this link deserves a word on method. The joint *block-increment* test — asking whether the entire dynamics block raises R² beyond chance — did not reach significance under the wild cluster bootstrap; but that test is a conservative, low-power procedure for a network with only ~12 domains, and it answers a different question from "which trends matter." When we estimate the individual coefficients in a Bayesian multilevel model that propagates the site- and domain-level uncertainty directly (0 divergences, R̂ ≤ 1.002), three structural-dynamics trends are **credibly** associated with nestedness — the canopy height-ratio trend (β = −0.31, 95% HDI [−0.42, −0.20]), the vertical-CV trend (+0.20 [+0.08, +0.32]) and the leaf-area trend (−0.19 [−0.31, −0.07]) — the largest credible block for this component. We therefore treat the dynamics → nestedness relationship not as a merely descriptive pattern but as a **coefficient-level supported result**, with the caveat that the conservative joint block test is underpowered at this network size; the honest reading is that the effect is real in these data and awaits a stiffer joint test in a network with more independent sites. That the signal is strongest for nestedness rather than turnover is itself informative, and it cuts against the intuition the terms invite: although "turnover" *sounds* like the dynamic component and "nestedness" like a static plot-to-plot difference, the remote-sensing signals map by the **nature of the underlying process, not by the connotation of the label** — lateral species replacement (turnover), a fundamentally spatial contrast between similarly-developed stands, is tracked by static structural *dissimilarity* (§4.3 below), whereas directional richness loss/gain (nestedness) is tracked by the *temporal trend*, because a stand shedding or accruing species relative to richer neighbours is on a developmental trajectory that its canopy dynamics record.

## 4.3 Remote-sensing heterogeneity tracks species replacement (the variation hypothesis)

Compositional turnover, invisible to every predictor *level* in the plot-level framework, became visible the moment the same observations were re-expressed as between-plot *dissimilarity*: plots more different in canopy structure or spectra held more different species (within-site Mantel r ≈ +0.3 for both, structural and spectral distance contributing non-redundantly in a joint saturation model; Results §3.1b). This is a structural-and-spectral generalization of the spectral variation hypothesis (Palmer et al. 2002; Rocchini et al. 2010), demonstrated here with biogeography held fixed by within-site permutation — so it is not an artifact of broad environmental gradients. The analytic lesson is as important as the ecological one: whether remote sensing "captures" beta diversity depends on how the question is posed. Asked as a regression of composition on canopy properties, the answer is essentially no; asked as a correspondence between dissimilarity matrices, the answer is yes. Monitoring designs that aim at species replacement should therefore operate on remote-sensing *distances* (as in generalized dissimilarity modelling) rather than per-plot predictions. At the same time, the signal is real but modest, and specifically confined to replacement (structural and spectral local uniqueness correlated with turnover but not with richness difference): much of local species replacement evidently rides on soil, microtopography, dispersal and stochastic assembly that the canopy does not record — an intrinsic ceiling, not a sensor deficiency, and the counterpart of the nestedness result of §4.2, with which it completes the two-channel reading of beta diversity: *dissimilarity for the lateral component, dynamics for the directional one*.

## 4.4 Realized productivity supports a monotonic species–energy relationship (O3)

Because we deliberately avoided greenness as a productivity proxy, the productivity results speak to energy availability rather than canopy density. All three independent GPP estimates — MODIS MOD17, PML-V2, and eddy-covariance tower GPP — showed positive species–energy relationships, and the gold-standard tower relationship was clear (Hill q1 vs tower GPP r = +0.59, p = 0.004, n = 22), anchored at its low end by the boreal-Alaska towers (~380 gC m⁻² yr⁻¹, roughly a quarter of the most productive temperate sites). The relationship was monotonic, with no evidence of a unimodal hump in either MODIS or the tower data — reinforcing our treatment of greenness-based "productivity humps" as artifacts of canopy density rather than energy. Two scale considerations shape how far we push this. First, the satellite GPP products are effectively site-scale (intra-class correlation ≈ 0.92), so we report species–energy at the site level rather than as a plot-level framework block. Second, when productivity *was* forced into the plot-level model its unique contribution was modest and, for turnover, an uninterpretable suppression artifact — MODIS and PML GPP took opposing signs despite r = 0.85 — which is itself the reason we keep productivity at the site scale. We therefore frame realized productivity as a genuine, energy-based corroboration of a monotonic species–energy pattern, distinct from and not competing at plot scale with the canopy-structure signal that dominates local diversity.

## 4.5 Context dependence and its limits in a site-sparse network (O4)

Context moderation proved to be the least resolvable layer of the framework — an informative outcome in itself. The productivity–diversity relationship was descriptively steeper in older stands (simple slopes rising monotonically with stand age), an attractive successional story, but the formal GPP × stand-age interaction did not survive multiple-testing correction (MODIS × age q = 0.26). We therefore report age-dependence as suggestive only, and draw the general lesson: **context-moderation effects estimated from a few dozen site clusters are fragile**, because interaction terms demand the between-site contrasts that a network of ~26 sites in ~12 domains can least afford; continental networks like NEON sit near the lower limit of what few-cluster-valid inference can support for interaction tests. The disturbance axis was more consistent: the structure–diversity slope declined monotonically from least- to most-disturbed plots (the FDR-surviving structural term being leaf-area index), and the before–after/control–impact analysis estimated a loss of roughly one species (p = 0.069) concentrated among rare species, with abundance-weighted diversity unchanged. Disturbance thus appears to genuinely erode the structure–diversity coupling and to prune rare species while leaving dominance structure intact — but the effect sizes are small and the disturbed sample modest, so we frame disturbance moderation as directionally supported rather than definitive.

## 4.6 Limitations

Three limitations bound our inference. First, **statistical power at the site level remains the central constraint**: even at 26 sites, several NEON domains contain a single site, so the wild-bootstrap *joint block* tests are conservative for beta components and for interactions, and some effects that are credible at the coefficient level (dynamics → nestedness, supported by the Bayesian multilevel model) or directionally consistent (disturbance moderation) do not clear the joint few-cluster increment test. Second, **covariate provenance is heterogeneous**: one site (TREE) required recovery from a shared AOP flight box, one (ORNL) lacks the bidirectional spectral product entirely, four lack processed tower GPP, and the GAMI age and land-cover products were extracted from current product versions; we validated the age extraction against an independent earlier pull (r = 0.83) and extracted all 26 sites from single sources to guarantee internal consistency, but cross-version comparability with earlier literature should be treated with care. Third, **the turnover signal, though robust, is weak**: as predictor levels no dimension carries much unique turnover information, and the dissimilarity signal of §4.3 is modest (r ≈ 0.3), so our strongest beta claims concern richness difference rather than replacement, and the plot-level productivity–turnover association in particular rests on a suppression pattern we decline to interpret directionally.

## 4.7 Implications for remote biodiversity monitoring

Four practical conclusions follow. (i) **Match the sensor — and the analytical framing — to the diversity dimension**: static LiDAR structure (as predictor levels) for local richness; repeat-LiDAR trends for richness difference; structural/spectral *dissimilarity* (not levels) for species replacement; independent GPP for the species–energy gradient. (ii) **Do not rely on spectral greenness alone** — it is the weakest of the three dimensions, informative only for alpha, and structural and spectral observations are not interchangeable. (iii) **Use realized productivity, not greenness**, when testing energy–diversity theory — the monotonic, no-hump relationship emerges cleanly only with GPP products independent of canopy density, and is best resolved across broad productivity gradients such as the temperate-to-boreal transition. (iv) **Treat context-moderation from sparse networks with scepticism**: interaction effects are the first casualties of few-cluster inference — in our own screen the age-moderation of the productivity signal fell short of multiple-testing correction despite consistent simple slopes — and such effects should be validated against larger site networks before they enter the ecological narrative.

# 5. Conclusions

Remote sensing does not observe forest biodiversity through a single window. Across 26 NEON sites spanning the temperate-to-boreal forests of North America, static canopy structure, multitemporal structural dynamics, and independently estimated ecosystem productivity encode **distinct and complementary** dimensions of tree diversity: structure carries the broadest signal and dominates local (alpha) diversity; structural dynamics contribute most uniquely — and, under Bayesian multilevel inference, credibly — to richness difference (nestedness); species replacement (turnover) is invisible to predictor levels but tracked by structural and spectral dissimilarity; and realized productivity supports a monotonic species–energy relationship that is corroborated by eddy-covariance measurements and is not reducible to canopy greenness. The signals map to the ecological processes underlying each diversity component — static contrast for lateral replacement, temporal trajectory for directional richness change — rather than to any single sensor's view. The division of biodiversity information among remote-sensing dimensions is therefore real and, we argue, transferable; the context-dependence of those relationships is more fragile than site-sparse networks can yet resolve. For biodiversity monitoring, the practical message is to combine complementary sensors and framings deliberately, to ground productivity–diversity inference in realized carbon uptake rather than greenness, and to hold context-moderation claims to the standard of replication across larger observational networks.
